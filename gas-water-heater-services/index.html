A gas water heater is a refreshingly simple machine, which is part of why it lasts. A burner under the tank heats the water, exhaust rises through a flue that runs up the center of the tank, and a gas control valve on the front acts as both thermostat and safety shutoff. That valve only lets gas reach the burner while it senses a flame, through a thermocouple on standing-pilot models or a flame sensor on electronic ignition models. When something goes wrong, it is usually at one of those points. In Williamsburg, VA, we regularly find thermocouples that no longer hold the valve open, pilots starved by a lint-clogged flame arrestor screen, gas valves that have failed internally, and flue or vent problems that let exhaust spill back into the room.

Every gas water heater call starts with safety and then moves to testing. If there is a gas odor, leave the home first, call the gas utility from outside, and do not operate switches or try to relight anything. Once the area is safe, our Williamsburg technicians work through the heater systematically: millivolt readings on the thermocouple, a close look at the pilot and main burner flames, gas pressure at the control valve, a draft test to make sure exhaust is not spilling back, and a CO check around the appliance. Scorch marks at the burner access, rust flakes on the burner, and a lazy yellow flame each point to a different problem, and we chase down the right one.

How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank
  • Hot water that runs out faster than it used to
  • A faint gas or rotten-egg smell near the heater (leave and call the gas utility)
  • A pilot that lights, then goes out when you release the knob
  • A tank that pops and bangs while it heats, a classic sign of sediment
  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater
  • A cold tank and a burner that will not come on

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Two things make gas water heater work a poor DIY project: the parts involve fuel and combustion, and the symptoms overlap. A pilot outage might come from a worn thermocouple, a partly blocked pilot tube, lint on the flame arrestor screen, a downdraft, or an internal valve failure. Guess wrong and you have spent money without fixing anything. Guess wrong on a gas fitting or vent connection and the stakes are much higher, since a small leak or a spillage problem can put gas or carbon monoxide into the living space. Gas valve and heater replacements also typically call for a permit.

Our technicians approach gas heaters with instruments rather than assumptions. They check thermocouple output in millivolts, read gas pressure at the valve, test draft at the hood, check for CO around the appliance, and leak-test every joint they touch. That process fixes the actual fault the first time and leaves your home safer than before. We also give you an honest assessment of the heater itself. A tank past about ten or twelve years with a failing valve or a leak is usually a replacement, and we will say so before you spend money on a repair.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

A few preventive steps can help you avoid a cold shower or a wet floor down the road. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Replace a spent anode rod before the tank itself starts to rust
  • Keep working CO alarms near bedrooms and on each floor
  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Keep the area around the heater clear, especially of gasoline, paint, and solvents
  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
