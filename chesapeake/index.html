How Chesapeake Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Many early failures trace back to skipped maintenance, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Flush the tank every year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and operate the relief valve annually so it doesn't seize. Set the thermostat close to 120 degrees for safer water and less scale. If you have a tankless unit, descale it yearly where the water is hard to protect the heat exchanger.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The serial number on the label usually encodes the month and year it was built, and that date says a lot about what comes next. Most tank models make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units around 20. If your heater is past that range and also shows rusty water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than a rushed emergency install after a leak.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to high water pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can keep stressing the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Common Water Heater Problems in Chesapeake

Water heaters in and around Chesapeake deal with a predictable set of stresses. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Age is the other big factor. Plenty of homes around here still have the original tank, and once a tank is past the ten-year mark, the anode rod has usually been consumed and corrosion begins attacking the steel itself. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Well water may bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank on closed systems, a correctly piped T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak would hit finished floors, and seismic straps where local code requires them. All of these is fixable, and most cost far less when caught early.
